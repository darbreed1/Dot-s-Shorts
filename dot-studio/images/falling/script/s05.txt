The International Space Station is falling right now. It has been for more than 25 years. So why doesn't it hit the ground? Up there, about 400 kilometres above us, gravity is still about 90% as strong as it is down here. If the station just stopped, it would come straight down. The trick is that it's also moving sideways. Very fast. About 8 kilometres every second. Picture throwing a ball. It curves down and lands. Throw it harder, and it lands further away. Throw it hard enough, and the ground curves away beneath it as fast as it falls. It keeps falling, and it keeps missing. That's an orbit. The station goes all the way around the Earth about every 90 minutes, 16 times a day. And the astronauts float, because they're falling too, at exactly the same rate as the station around them. Even so, at that height, there's still a tiny bit of air, and it slowly drags the station lower. So every now and then, an engine gives it a push to climb back up. Around 2030, NASA plans to stop pushing and steer it down into a remote part of the ocean.

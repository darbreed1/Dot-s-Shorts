Jump out of a plane, and how fast do you end up going? You'd think the higher you start, the faster you'll be by the end. But that's not how it works. When you first jump, gravity speeds you up quickly. But the faster you go, the harder the air pushes back. After about 12 seconds, the push of the air matches the pull of gravity, and you stop speeding up. That top speed is called terminal velocity. Lying flat on your belly the way most skydivers fall, it's about 200 kilometres an hour. Turn head down so you're narrow like a dart, and there's less air in the way, about 240 to 290. Speed skydivers push this as far as it goes. They jump from high up, where the air is thinner, tuck in tight, and point straight down. In August 2025, Spain's Sebastián García Gutiérrez set the official record, an average of about 537 kilometres an hour across a whole competition. At the World Championships this October, he went faster still, though that's still waiting to be made official. So once you've been falling for 10 seconds or so, falling further doesn't make you any faster. The air sets your speed, not the height.

How does a parachute turn a 200 kilometre an hour fall into a gentle landing? It doesn't stop you. It gives the air something much bigger to push against. Here's what happens, in order. At the right height, a skydiver throws a small pilot chute into the wind. It catches the air, pulls a pin, opens the container on their back, and drags out a bag with the main parachute folded inside. The lines stretch out, the bag lets go, and the canopy fills with air. A small square of fabric called a slider sits on the lines and slows that opening down, so it doesn't jolt you too hard. And modern canopies aren't domes. They're wings, made of cells that fill with air, so a skydiver can steer and slow right down before touching the ground. Most of the time, it all works. But on roughly one jump in a thousand, something goes wrong. The lines can twist. The canopy can open lopsided, or get stuck in its bag. That's why every skydiver carries two parachutes. If the main one fails, they pull one handle to cut it away, and another to open the reserve. In the US, that reserve has to be checked and repacked by a certified rigger every 180 days, even if it's never used. And in case a skydiver can't pull at all, many rigs carry a small computer that keeps track of height and speed. If they're still falling fast, too close to the ground, it fires and opens the reserve by itself. So a parachute isn't one clever piece of fabric. It's a chain of backups.

## Voiceover

### 0. Intro
Everything falls. Raindrops, skydivers, and, right now, the International Space Station, with people on board. Falling sounds simple. You let go, and you go down. But look a little closer and it gets strange. There's a speed you can't go past. There's a famous record that might not be true. There's a piece of fabric that turns a fall into a soft landing. And there's a space station that's been falling for more than twenty-five years without ever hitting the ground. So let's find out what actually happens when you fall.

### 1. How fast do you actually fall?
Jump out of a plane, and how fast do you end up going? You'd think the higher you start, the faster you'll be by the end. But that's not how it works. When you first jump, gravity speeds you up quickly. But the faster you go, the harder the air pushes back. After about twelve seconds, the push of the air matches the pull of gravity, and you stop speeding up. That top speed is called terminal velocity. Lying flat on your belly, the way most skydivers fall, it's about two hundred kilometres an hour. Turn head-down, so you're narrow like a dart, and there's less air in the way: about two hundred and forty to two hundred and ninety. Speed skydivers push this as far as it goes. They jump from high up, where the air is thinner, tuck in tight and point straight down. In August twenty twenty-five, Spain's Sebastián García Gutiérrez set the official record: an average of about five hundred and thirty-seven kilometres an hour across a whole competition. At the world championships this October he went faster still, though that's still waiting to be made official. So once you've been falling for ten seconds or so, falling further doesn't make you any faster. The air sets your speed, not the height.

### 2. The record that might not be true
What's the highest fall anyone has ever survived without a parachute? According to Guinness World Records, it's ten thousand, one hundred and sixty metres. In January nineteen seventy-two, a Yugoslav airliner broke apart in the sky over what was then Czechoslovakia. The official investigation blamed a bomb. Twenty-eight people were on board, and twenty-seven of them died. The one survivor was a twenty-two-year-old flight attendant called Vesna Vulović. A man from a nearby village found her trapped in the wreckage, badly injured. She was in a coma for three days, and it took her months to walk again. But she lived, and her fall, from the plane's cruising height, went into the record books. Then, in two thousand and nine, two journalists challenged that number. They argued the plane actually broke up much lower, maybe only a few hundred metres up, after a fast emergency descent. They pointed to how small an area the wreckage covered, and to people on the ground who saw it come down. They even suggested the plane might have been shot down by mistake, and that the high-altitude bomb story was invented to hide it. But one of the journalists admitted the evidence was only circumstantial, and it has never been proved. Guinness still lists the record. So was it ten kilometres, or a few hundred metres? Either way, she survived something almost nobody survives. We just may never know how far she really fell.

### 3. How a parachute works, and how it can fail
How does a parachute turn a two-hundred-kilometre-an-hour fall into a gentle landing? It doesn't stop you. It gives the air something much bigger to push against. Here's what happens, in order. At the right height, a skydiver throws a small pilot chute into the wind. It catches the air, pulls a pin, opens the container on their back, and drags out a bag with the main parachute folded inside. The lines stretch out, the bag lets go, and the canopy fills with air. A small square of fabric called a slider sits on the lines and slows that opening down, so it doesn't jolt you too hard. And modern canopies aren't domes. They're wings, made of cells that fill with air, so a skydiver can steer, and slow right down before touching the ground. Most of the time, it all works. But on roughly one jump in a thousand, something goes wrong. The lines can twist. The canopy can open lopsided, or get stuck in its bag. That's why every skydiver carries two parachutes. If the main one fails, they pull one handle to cut it away, and another to open the reserve. In the US, that reserve has to be checked and repacked by a certified rigger every hundred and eighty days, even if it's never used. And in case a skydiver can't pull at all, many rigs carry a small computer that keeps track of height and speed. If they're still falling fast too close to the ground, it fires and opens the reserve by itself. So a parachute isn't one clever piece of fabric. It's a chain of backups.

### 4. Why the space station is falling right now
The International Space Station is falling right now. It has been for more than twenty-five years. So why doesn't it hit the ground? Up there, about four hundred kilometres above us, gravity is still about ninety percent as strong as it is down here. If the station just stopped, it would come straight down. The trick is that it's also moving sideways, very fast: about eight kilometres every second. Picture throwing a ball. It curves down and lands. Throw it harder, and it lands further away. Throw it hard enough, and the ground curves away beneath it as fast as it falls. It keeps falling, and it keeps missing. That's an orbit. The station goes all the way around the Earth about every ninety minutes, sixteen times a day. And the astronauts float because they're falling too, at exactly the same rate as the station around them. Even so, at that height there's still a tiny bit of air, and it slowly drags the station lower. So every now and then, an engine gives it a push to climb back up. Around twenty thirty, NASA plans to stop pushing, and steer it down into a remote part of the ocean.

### 5. Outro
So that's what actually happens when you fall. You speed up, until the air won't let you go any faster. A record can be harder to pin down than it sounds. A few square metres of fabric, and a lot of backups, can bring you down softly. And if you move sideways fast enough, you can keep falling for decades and never land. Everything is falling. Some things are just very good at missing.

  ratified record is his competition average, 536.75 km/h (same event). At the October 2026 world championships in
  Eloy he won again and reportedly went faster (540.59 km/h average per a flight-school report), but those records
  are pending FAI ratification, so the script says "faster still, though that's still waiting to be made official".

  one journalist conceded the evidence was "only circumstantial". Unverified second-hand claims (official
  dismissals, Vulović's reaction) are left out.

# Why don't wild orcas kill people? (long-form, ~7 min)

- **Content type:** Explainer / fun fact (animals), long-form 16:9
- **Prompted by:** a transcript the user pasted from the YouTube channel Primal Phase (orcas and humans). The script
  below is independent research and original wording on the same question, not a rework of that video. Several
  claims in that transcript were wrong or overstated and are NOT used (see Notes).
- **Idea:** Orcas kill blue whales and great white sharks, yet there's no confirmed case of a wild orca killing a
  person. Three clues (learned food traditions, echolocation, a careful social brain), the captivity deaths that
  prove the point, and an honest "we don't fully know".
- **Word count:** 870 (about 6 minutes at Dot's pace)

## Voiceover

### 1. Hook
Orcas hunt great white sharks. They hunt blue whales, the biggest animals that have ever lived. And yet, as far as anyone knows, a wild orca has never killed a person. Not one confirmed case. People swim, surf, kayak and dive near them all over the world. So why do the ocean's top predators leave us alone? The honest answer is that scientists aren't completely sure. But the clues are fascinating.

### 2. What an orca is
First, the name is a bit misleading. A killer whale is actually a dolphin, the biggest dolphin there is. A big male can grow to about nine metres long and weigh around six tonnes, as much as an elephant. Females can live for eighty years or more. Orcas live in every ocean, from warm seas to the ice around Antarctica, in tight family groups called pods, often led by an old mother, a grandmother.

### 3. The hunters
And they are very good hunters. Off the south coast of Western Australia, researchers watched pods of orcas kill blue whales three times, including a healthy adult. The orcas took turns tiring the whale out, and some pressed down on it, so it couldn't get up to breathe. With great white sharks, orcas seem to want one thing: the liver, which is huge and full of energy-rich oil. Off South Africa, sharks have washed up with their livers neatly removed. And when orcas show up, great whites often leave the area for weeks or even months. So this is not a gentle animal. This is the ocean's top predator.

### 4. The record
So what about us? There's one famous close call. In 1972, a surfer in California was bitten on the leg by an orca and needed about a hundred stitches. Scientists think it mistook him for a seal, and it let go. In recent years, a group of orcas off Spain and Portugal has been pushing sailing boats and breaking their rudders. But nobody has been killed. Orcas have had countless chances, and the number of people they've killed in the wild, as far as we know, is zero.

### 5. Clue one: family food rules
Clue number one: orcas have food traditions. In the North Pacific, scientists have found different kinds of orcas sharing the same waters. Resident orcas eat fish, mostly Chinook salmon. Bigg's orcas, sometimes called transients, hunt seals, sea lions and porpoises. Offshore orcas eat fish too, including sharks. They don't mix, they don't breed with each other, and they even call in different dialects. And their menu is learned. Calves learn what to eat, and how to catch it, from their mothers and their pod. It's so fixed that fish-eating residents, even when salmon is scarce, don't switch to the seals swimming right past them. Humans have never been on any orca family's menu. So nobody teaches it.

### 6. Clue two: we sound strange
Clue number two: sound. Orcas find food by clicking and listening to the echoes, which is called echolocation. It's so precise that residents may even tell kinds of salmon apart by the echo from a fish's swim bladder, the little air bag that helps it float. A person probably sounds nothing like their usual food. We're a different shape, we move differently, and divers come with wetsuits, metal tanks and bubbles. So to an orca, we may not register as food at all. Just as something strange.

### 7. Clue three: a careful brain
Clue number three: the brain. An orca's brain weighs around five to six kilograms, about four times as much as ours. Brain scans show it's deeply folded, with large areas thought to be linked to emotion and social life. Orcas are curious and cautious, and they learn from each other. When something big and unfamiliar shows up, the sensible choice is to check it out, and leave it alone.

### 8. What captivity changes
But here's the strange part. Orcas have killed people. Four times. And every one of those deaths happened in captivity, at marine parks. Three of them involved the same orca, a male called Tilikum. He was captured off Iceland in 1983 when he was about two years old, and spent the rest of his life in tanks, with orcas that weren't his family. Many scientists think life in a tank, with no family, no space and nothing like a wild orca's day, can lead to stress and aggression. So the captive record doesn't contradict the wild one. It shows what can go wrong when you take a clever, social animal away from everything it knows.

### 9. So why, really?
So why don't wild orcas kill us? Put the clues together. We're not on their learned menu. We probably sound strange, not tasty. And they're careful animals that don't attack things they don't understand. Even so, researchers who spend their lives with orcas admit they can't fully explain it. Maybe it's a mix of all three. Maybe it's something we haven't figured out yet.

### 10. Ending
And while orcas have left us alone, we haven't always returned the favour. In the 1960s and 70s, dozens of young orcas were captured off the Pacific Northwest for marine parks. Today the Southern Resident orcas who live there number only about seventy-five, and they're endangered, partly because their salmon has become scarce. So the next time you hear "killer whale", remember: the ocean's top predator has had every chance to hurt us, and it hasn't.
